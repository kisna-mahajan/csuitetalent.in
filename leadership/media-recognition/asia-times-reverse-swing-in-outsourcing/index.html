Context

Beyond Cost Arbitrage — India's Second Outsourcing Story

By early 2005, outsourcing had become one of the defining political and economic issues in the United States and Europe. The public debate focused almost entirely on jobs leaving developed economies. This Asia Times feature captured a more complex and ultimately more significant reality: outsourcing was creating a reverse swing — drawing talent and investment back into India in ways that had not been anticipated.

The article documented how Indian technology giants were expanding globally and how foreign professionals were increasingly seeking employment in India as high-value work — research, engineering, senior management — moved eastward.

From the Interview

Observations from Anil Mahajane

"Earlier, only call-center jobs were being outsourced to India. But now, as companies start to ship high-end research and senior managerial jobs as well to India, foreign workers see a huge opportunity for themselves here. Till recently, we were getting regular job queries from expatriate Indians wanting to move back to India. We were taken by surprise when overseas professionals from countries as far as the US, UK and South Africa also started calling us up to enquire about job opportunities here. This has now become a trend." — Anil Mahajane, Asia Times, February 2005
From the Article

Indian IT Goes Global — Creating Jobs in Both Directions

Infosys Consulting — A Case Study in Reverse Outsourcing

Infosys Technologies, India's second-largest software maker, reversed the outsourcing narrative by investing US$20 million to create nearly 500 consulting jobs in the United States. Its new subsidiary in Fremont, California — Infosys Consulting — was "aggressively hiring in America", with CEO Nandan Nilekani stating: "As we are looking to expand our global footprint, we are creating local employment in the countries we operate."

The article also noted a structural shift in how Indian IT companies approached Western markets. Previously restricted largely to Indian employees, firms such as Infosys, Wipro and TCS had begun hiring Western professionals to penetrate markets more effectively — a further sign that the talent dynamic between India and the West was becoming bidirectional.

The Evolving Landscape

From Back-Office to Knowledge Economy

The first wave of outsourcing to India was driven by cost: call centres, customer support, back-office processing. By 2005, a second wave was well underway — one defined by knowledge, innovation and leadership rather than cost alone:

  • Research and Development centres established by global multinationals
  • Product engineering and software development at scale
  • Senior management and global delivery leadership roles
  • Consulting and advisory functions
  • Financial services and analytics operations

This transition fundamentally changed India's profile in the global economy — and made it an increasingly attractive destination for experienced international professionals, not just a source of cost savings.

Historical Significance

The Trend That Defined India's Next Two Decades

When Anil Mahajane described a "reverse swing" in 2005, it was an observation at the frontier of a much larger transformation. The trajectory he described has since become one of India's defining economic characteristics.
